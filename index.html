Panpipes

← All projects

Read the README

A low raft of panpipes in 3mm plywood: eight closed pipes from E3 to E4, the E natural minor scale, side by side between two plates. You blow across the open tops, one pipe at a time. Each pipe is stopped by a plug pressed up from its open bottom, and moving the plug tunes it.

The same pipes also come as separate tubes glued side to side, in four shapes: octagons in a row, octagons in two rows as a siku's are, hexagons in a zigzag, and hexagons in a row. The tube sets describes them.

Output is millimetre-true — 1 user unit = 1mm with a physical width/height — so it prints and cuts at real size.

It has not been cut or played yet. The pipe lengths come from the quarter-wave formula with a textbook end correction, and the player's lip changes that correction. The plugs are there to absorb it: Before you cut says by how much.

The panpipes

Notes E3 F♯3 G3 A3 B3 C4 D4 E4, the E natural minor scale, low to high
Bore rectangular, 20mm front to back in every pipe, 22mm across the lowest narrowing to 16mm across the highest
Raft 179mm across, 26mm deep, 538.8mm down the longest pipe
Parts 35: two plates, nine walls and 24 plug layers, on three sheets of 3mm ply; the strip edge adds a strip of 1.5mm stock
Joints press-fit throughout, sealed with shellac; only the strip edge is glued

Why it stops at E3. A closed pipe sounds a quarter wavelength, so E3 needs about 518mm of air, and the pipe is cut 21mm longer again for its plug and the room to move it. D3 would need a pipe about 603mm long, and the parts must fit a 600 × 305mm bed with a margin round them.

Why the pipes narrow. Low pipes want a wide bore to sound full, and high ones a narrow bore to speak easily: a pipe that is short for its width plays breathy. Every pipe here is at least 12.7 times as long as the diameter of the circle of its area. The depth, front to back, stays at 20mm so the plates stay flat.

The tops are flush, so the rim is one straight line to blow along; the bottoms step down with the pipes. Each pipe has, from the rim down:

  • The air column, from the rim to the plug's face: a quarter wavelength less the open end's correction, 0.61 times the radius of the circle with the bore's area.
  • The plug: three layers of ply, each 0.12mm bigger than the bore both ways, the square flute's press-fit for its plug discs. A blue line across the front plate marks where its face goes.
  • 12mm of empty pipe below the plug, so it can be pushed down to flatten the note as well as up to sharpen it.

How it goes together. The front and back plates are the faces; nine walls stand between them, one between each pair of pipes and one at each end. An inner wall has 6mm tabs about 40mm apart, through slots in both plates. The two end walls have the square flute's finger joint, a 6mm finger every 12mm, in notches in the plates' long edges, flush with the outside. An inner wall is as long as the longer of its two pipes, so it runs on past the shorter one's bottom, beside the plates' staircase edge; its tabs there are fingers in notches in that edge.

The blowing edge

A panpipe speaks when a thin jet of breath, aimed across the open top, strikes the far rim, the top of the back plate, and flips in and out of the pipe. The far rim is the pipe's only edge, and its sharpness sets the tone: a sharp edge gives a clear, strong note that starts easily, and a blunt one a breathier note that needs the jet aimed more exactly. Cane panpipes have walls 1 to 2mm thick. A laser cuts 3mm ply square, which is at the blunt end of what plays.

The near rim, the front plate's top, is where the lower lip rests. It is not an edge and does not need to be sharp. In every build, sand it round so it is comfortable.

The generator draws four blowing edges, chosen with --edge=; each has its own cut files and its own view in 3D. They differ only in the back plate and, for strip, a fourth sheet.

chamfer, the default: sand a bevel

The back plate is cut square, with a blue line engraved across its outside face 2mm below the rim. Sand a 45° bevel on the outside of the rim, down to the line, leaving 1mm of edge on the inside of the bore. The tops are flush, so a sanding block held at 45° along the whole raft does every pipe at once, after assembly.

This is the one to start with: no extra parts, the laser marks where to stop, and a 1mm edge is in the cane pipes' range. Sand until the line just disappears, and no further; the inside face must stay untouched, since that is the edge.

plain: the edge as cut

The back plate as it comes off the laser, a 3mm square edge. It plays: wooden panpipes drilled from a block have thick, rounded rims. Expect a breathier tone, most of all in the short high pipes, and a narrower window of aim. Cut this one if you want to hear the difference a bevel makes, and chamfer it afterwards: it is the same plate without the line.

notch: a quena's notch over every pipe

A U-shaped notch is cut into the back plate's rim over each pipe, half the pipe's width across and up to 6.5mm deep, and a blue line is engraved round each one on the back plate's outside face, 2mm out from the notch's edge. File a 45° bevel on the outside of each notch, down to its line, leaving 1mm of edge on the inside of the bore. That is a quena's notch: a U in the rim, bevelled from outside so the edge the breath strikes is thin. A small round file, or sandpaper round a dowel, follows the U.

The notch gives the jet a definite place to aim, and the bevel the thin edge, so this build has both. It is the most hand work of the four: eight small curved bevels in place of one straight one. File until the line just disappears, and keep the file off the inside face, which is the edge.

Notched panpipes are borrowed from the quena, not traditional: siku and nai pipes have plain rims. The notch removes some of the back wall at the top of each pipe, which shortens the pipe a little acoustically; the plugs take that up.

strip: a thin edge glued on

The back plate stops 5mm short of the rim, and a strip of 1.5mm stock, 5mm tall and the raft's full width, is glued along its top edge, inner face flush with the bore, so the rim is 1.5mm thick. The walls run up past the plate to the rim, and the strip glues to their back edges as well. It is cut from its own sheet, panpipes-E3-strip-1.5mm-sheet1-cut-files.svg, in 1.5mm ply or veneer.

This gives the thinnest edge without sanding, at the cost of a second material and a glued joint, the one glued joint in any raft build. The strip has no joints of its own, so its kerf hardly matters.

The tube sets

tube_panpipes.py builds the same eight pipes another way: each pipe its own tube of 3mm strips, finger-jointed edge to edge as the octagonal flute's tube is, and the tubes glued to each other face to face. Separate tubes share no wall, so the glue is what holds the set together; inside each tube the joints are press-fit and sealed with shellac, as everywhere else. Each bore has the same area as the raft's pipe for the same note, 440mm² at E3 down to 320mm² at E4, so the air columns, the plugs' lines and the tuning are the raft's. Before you cut applies to the tubes too: the stock, the kerf and the end correction are the same.

Each is four sheets of 3mm ply, the longest strip 538.9mm.

octagon-row is the closest to the raft: a flat to the front for the lip, a flat to the back for the blowing edge, and a flat either side to glue to the neighbours. It has the most strips, 64, and the narrowest faces, 10.6 to 12.0mm outside.

octagon-two-rows is the siku's layout. The Andean siku splits its scale between two rows, one behind the other, so neighbouring notes alternate between them and the instrument is half as wide. Here the front row is E3, G3, B3 and D4 and the back row F♯3, A3, C4 and E4, each back pipe directly behind the note below it. Each row is glued in a line, flat to flat, and the back row is glued to the back of the front row, flat to flat again; the rims are level, as a siku's are. The set is 110.5mm across, half the octagon row. Each back pipe is a little narrower than the one in front of it, so the back row is slid to split the difference both ways, and the faces glued between the rows share at least 5.6mm of flat face.

Gluing the rows together changes two details. A back-row pipe's front is glued out of sight, so its plug's blue line is on its back instead. And a front-row pipe's back face, its blowing edge, is glued to the back row, so it can take no line and no notch: on a chamfer build, sand its bevel across the joint, leaving 1mm of the front pipe's wall flat at the rim and sloping down at 45° through both walls. The slope reaches the back pipe's bore 5mm below its rim, lowering the near rim of the back pipe, where the lip rests to play it. The notch edge is not drawn for this shape.

hex-zigzag turns each hexagon so it has a flat to the front and one to the back, and then its sides are corners, so it cannot sit flat beside its neighbour in a line. Instead each pipe is glued to the next across a diagonal face, and the pipes alternate between a front row and a back row 14mm behind it: E3, G3, B3 and D4 in front, F♯3, A3, C4 and E4 behind, the scale split as a siku's is, but with the rows nested into each other's gaps rather than one whole row behind the other. The rims are level. --raise= stands the back row taller at the rim, --raise=15 by 15mm, to bring each back pipe's front face clear above the front row for the lip; that is an idea of this design, not a tradition, and nobody has played either yet. Each pipe is glued to two neighbours, on faces 14.6 to 16.5mm wide, 9.4mm of it clear of the joints.

hex-row turns the hexagons the other way, flats either side, and glues them in a line. A corner faces the lip, so round it off with sandpaper, and the blowing edge is a V of two back faces rather than one flat. That is the least conventional of the four to play.

The edges are the raft's, on the back face of every tube:

  • chamfer, the default: a line engraved 2mm below the rim on the back face, on both back faces for hex-row. Sand a 45° bevel down to it. The rims are at one height, but the back faces are not in line, since the tubes narrow, so bevel each tube on its own. On octagon-two-rows only the back row has the line; the front row's bevels go across the joint, as above.
  • plain: the rim as cut.
  • notch: the quena notch with its bevel line, narrower than the raft's to fit a single face: 4.9 to 5.7mm wide and 3.4 to 3.9mm deep on the octagons, 6.7 to 7.8mm wide and 4.3 to 4.9mm deep on the hexagons. The back face keeps its whole width for 18mm below the rim, its neighbours' fingers moved clear, so the notch is never next to a joint. There is no notch on hex-row, which has a corner at the back, or on octagon-two-rows, whose front row's backs are glued.

Building a tube set

  1. Sort the strips by length: each length is one tube, eight strips for an octagon, six for a hexagon. A tube's strips are not all alike: the fingers along their edges come in three patterns, so each strip closes against only its own neighbours. Start from the front, the strip whose blue line is 21mm from one end, the bottom; work round it, each strip's fingers into the last one's gaps, and if a strip will not close, it belongs elsewhere in the ring. The back is the strip with the line 2mm below the rim on a chamfer build (two strips on hex-row), or with the U on a notch build. On octagon-two-rows the back row's plug lines are on their backs, so there the strip with both lines, or with the plug's line alone on a plain build, is the back. Rubber bands hold a tube while you work round it.
  2. Glue the tubes to each other before any shellac, since glue will not take on shellac. Stand them rim down on something flat, so the rims come out level, and glue each to the next in order, low to high, the fronts all facing you. For octagon-two-rows, glue each row in a line first, then the back row to the back of the front row, each back pipe behind the note below it. On a hex-zigzag drawn with --raise=, the back row's rims stand past the front row's, so put a spacer that thick under the front row's rims.
  3. Seal every seam with high-cut shellac, outside and inside each tube, from both ends.
  4. Make the blowing edge, round the lip side of each rim, and shellac the rims.
  5. Press the plugs in from below to their lines, and tune as for the raft, below.

Turn it in 3D

Before you cut

The end correction is a textbook figure, and the lip changes it. The pipes are cut for 0.61 times the bore's equivalent radius, an unflanged open end's. Your lower lip covers part of the opening and the raft's own width flanges it, and both make the real correction different. The whole range from 0.3 to 0.61 moves the plugs 3.1 to 3.7mm, and every plug has 12mm of pipe below it to come down into, and as far as it likes to go up. So the plugs can absorb it whichever way it falls.

How far a plug tunes. A millimetre of plug moves the note 3.3 cents in the E3 pipe and 6.6 cents in the E4 pipe. Pushed all the way down, into its 12mm, a plug flattens its pipe by 39 cents (E3) to 77 cents (E4), and pushed up it sharpens it as far as you like. To tune for another temperament or A, redraw with --notes= or move the plugs.

Cut it in 3mm Baltic birch plywood, at a 0.13mm kerf. The tabs and fingers are 6mm wide with no play, so the fit comes from the kerf compensation alone. If your laser's kerf is not 0.13mm, redraw the sheets for its own with --kerf=: a press-fit that comes out loose wants a wider kerf, one too tight to close a narrower one.

The longest parts are 538.9mm with the kerf, the front plate, the back plate and the end wall beside the lowest pipe. The largest sheet is 600 × 304mm, so the bed must be the full 600 × 305mm. The generator checks against it.

Run the engrave first, then the cuts. Blue engraves the plugs' places on the front plate and, on the chamfer and notch builds, the bevel's lines on the back plate. Then orange cuts the slots, and black frees the parts. Both plates are drawn outside face up, so the lines land on the outside. The tube sets have no slots: blue engraves the plugs' places and the bevels' lines on the strips' outsides, and black frees the parts.

Building it

  1. For the strip edge only: glue the strip along the back plate's cut-down top edge, inner faces flush, and let it dry. Every other edge is made after assembly.
  2. Press the nine walls into the back plate, tabs through the slots, its outside face down. The two end walls go in the notches along its long edges, the longer one beside the E3 pipe. Each inner wall fits only its own place, since every wall is a different length.
  3. Lay the front plate on, outside face up, so its blue lines show, and press it down onto the walls' tabs. Rubber bands round the raft hold it while you work along.
  4. Seal every seam with high-cut shellac, outside and inside as far as a brush on a stick reaches down each pipe from both ends. A closed pipe that leaks will not speak at its bottom note.
  5. Make the blowing edge: for chamfer, sand the bevel down to the line; for notch, file each notch's bevel down to its line. Round the front rim where the lip rests in every build, and shellac both rims, which are end grain.
  6. Once the shellac is dry, press each pipe's three plug layers up into it from the bottom, one at a time, with a dowel, until the top one's face is at the pipe's blue line. Sealing first keeps shellac off the plugs, which have to stay free to move.

Tuning

Blow each pipe and read its pitch with a tuner. Flat: push the plug up. Sharp: push it down. Move it a millimetre at a time; at 3.3 to 6.6 cents a millimetre, a millimetre is a lot.

A plug that has gone too far up comes back down by pushing from the top with a long dowel. If a plug works loose, shellac on its edges, left to dry before it goes back in, tightens it.

Regenerating it

python3 panpipes.py --no-write                  # the checks, writing nothing

python3 panpipes.py --notes=G3,A3,B3,C4,D4,E4,F#4,G4 --no-write   # other pipes, low to high
python3 panpipes.py --end=0.4 --no-write        # a different open-end correction
python3 panpipes.py --kerf=0.18 --out=mine      # for a laser with a 0.18mm kerf
python3 panpipes.py --page-to=turn.html         # the 3D view, all four edges

python3 tube_panpipes.py --page-to=turn-tubes.html   # the tube sets' 3D view

panpipes.py draws nothing until every check passes:

  • every blue line on the front is its plug's face;
  • the walls stand a bore apart;
  • every plug is a press-fit in its bore;
  • every plug can come down as well as go up;
  • every pipe is walled to its bottom;
  • the rim is clear of every joint;
  • every slot keeps ply between it and the plate's edge;
  • no tab straddles a pipe's bottom, which it reports only when one does;
  • on the notch build, every notch stays within its pipe and clear of the joints;
  • on the notch build, every notch has its bevel line, clear of the joints;
  • on the chamfer build, the bevel leaves an edge inside the bore;
  • no pipe is fat for its length;
  • the longest part fits the bed;
  • every sheet fits the bed;
  • every part is on a sheet.

tube_panpipes.py checks that every blue line is its plug's face, on a face no neighbour is glued over, that every bore is the area the pipe is tuned for, that every plug is a press-fit and can come down as well as go up, that every glued face lies flat on its neighbour's and no two tubes overlap, that a face keeps ply between its two joints, that every notch and its bevel line fit the back face or every free back face has its bevel line, and the same pipe, part and sheet checks.

panpipes.py imports the square flute's kerf, finger joint and packer from square_flute.py, and the mitred offset from the four-hole ocarinas' four_hole_ocarinas.py; tube_panpipes.py also takes the tube's joint from their polyhedron.py. So square-flute, four-hole-ocarinas, swept-curve and lasermade-tools must be checked out beside this repository.
